1.1 What is Intelligence & Artificial Intelligence?

Good morning class. When we begin our journey into Artificial Intelligence, the fundamental question we must pose is: what actually constitutes intelligence?

Intelligence is defined as the capability to calculate, reason, perceive relationships and analogies, learn from experience, store and retrieve information from memory, solve problems, comprehend complex ideas, use natural language fluently, classify, generalize, and adapt to novel situations.

In concise technical terms from our lecture syllabus: "Intelligence is the ability to think and achieve desired goals, thus we can say intelligence is used for PROBLEM SOLVING."

Traditionally, intelligence was viewed as a uniquely human biological trait. When this intelligence—specifically the perception, learning, and decision-making ability—is engineered and implemented inside computational machines, it is designated as ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE (AI).

Exam Definition to Write:
"Artificial Intelligence is that branch of computer science which focuses on creating software and hardware systems capable of demonstrating behaviors, reasoning, and problem-solving abilities that would be characterized as intelligent if exhibited by a human being."

1.2 The Four Approaches to Defining Artificial Intelligence

In historical literature and contemporary research, definitions of AI fall along two orthogonal dimensions:

  1. Thought processes and reasoning versus Observable behavior and action.
  2. Measuring success against Human performance (fidelity to human behavior) versus measuring against an ideal standard called Rationality (an idealized standard of doing the 'right thing').

Deep-Dive: The Turing Test (1950)

Proposed by Alan Turing in his landmark paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, the Turing Test provides an operational definition of intelligence based on indistinguishability.

  • Test Setup: A human interrogator interacts with two concealed entities—one human and one machine—via teletype / text terminal only.
  • Success Condition: If the interrogator cannot tell whether the written responses come from the person or the computer, the computer passes.
  • Six Required Capabilities to Pass the Full (Total) Turing Test:
    1. Natural Language Processing (NLP): To communicate fluently in natural human languages.
    2. Knowledge Representation (KR): To store what it knows or hears systematically.
    3. Automated Reasoning: To use stored information to answer questions and draw new conclusions.
    4. Machine Learning (ML): To adapt to new circumstances and detect and extrapolate patterns.
    5. Computer Vision: (Total Turing Test) To perceive physical objects and scenes.
    6. Robotics: (Total Turing Test) To manipulate physical objects and navigate the physical environment.

Deep-Dive: Cognitive Modeling (Thinking Humanly)

To construct machines that think like humans, we must understand the actual cognitive mechanisms of the brain. The 3 scientific methodologies are:

  • Introspection: Catching our own thoughts as they pass through our conscious mind.
  • Psychological Experiments: Observing human subjects solving logic or puzzle problems and recording response times and errors.
  • Brain Imaging (Neuroscience): Observing brain activity in real time via fMRI, PET, or EEG scans during cognitive tasks.
  • Key Landmark: Allen Newell and Herbert Simon developed the General Problem Solver (GPS) (1957) specifically to demonstrate that a program could follow the identical reasoning trace as a human test subject.

Deep-Dive: The Laws of Thought (Thinking Rationally)

The Greek philosopher Aristotle was among the first to formalize 'right thinking'—irrefutable reasoning processes called syllogisms:

Premise 1: Socrates is a man. Premise 2: All men are mortal. Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

These laws of thought initiated formal logic. However, this approach faces two major obstacles in practice: (1) It is difficult to take informal real-world knowledge and formulate it in the formal terms of logical notation, especially when knowledge is less than 100% certain; (2) There is a big difference between solving a problem in principle and solving it in practice (combinatorial explosion).

1.3 AI vs Machine Learning vs Deep Learning

Students frequently confuse AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning in viva and written exams. They are not competing alternatives—they are strictly nested concentric subsets:

  • Artificial Intelligence: The overarching discipline aiming to build smart machines capable of performing tasks requiring human intelligence.
  • Machine Learning: A branch of AI focusing on the development of mathematical algorithms that learn from historical training data without being explicitly programmed with hardcoded rules for every scenario.
  • Deep Learning: A specialized subfield of ML inspired by biological neural networks. Utilizes multi-layer artificial neural networks (DNNs, CNNs, Transformers) to extract features directly from raw high-dimensional data (pixels, audio waveforms, text).

The Core Trio: Modeling, Inference, and Learning

Every intelligent agent system relies on three interconnected pillars:

  1. Modeling: The mathematical or logical representation of the environment, rules, and relationships (e.g., transition models, Bayesian networks, logic clauses).
  2. Inference: Deriving conclusions, predictions, or action selections from the current model and observed facts (e.g., Modus Ponens, resolution, forward search).
  3. Learning: Updating the model itself when new evidence, errors, or environmental rewards become available.

1.5 Intelligent Agents & The Concept of Rationality

An agent is anything that can be viewed as perceiving its environment through sensors and acting upon that environment through actuators.

Mathematically, the agent's behavior is described by an Agent Function:

f: P* → A where P* represents the history of all percept sequences received to date, and A is the action chosen.

The Agent Program is the concrete implementation of this function running on physical computing architecture:

Agent = Architecture + Program

The Four Determinants of Rationality

A rational agent is one that does the right thing. But what is rational at any given instant? Rationality is NOT omniscience (knowing all future outcomes). Rationality depends strictly on four factors:

  1. The Performance Measure: The objective criterion defining success (specified externally by the designer).
  2. Prior Knowledge: The built-in knowledge the agent possesses about its environment.
  3. Available Actions: The finite set of actions the agent is physically capable of performing.
  4. The Percept Sequence to Date: The complete recorded sensory history of what the agent has experienced so far.
Core Definition to Memorize:
"For each possible percept sequence, a rational agent should select an action that is expected to maximize its performance measure, given the evidence provided by the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge the agent has."

1.6 The PEAS Description of Task Environments

Before designing an AI system, an engineer must first fully characterize its task environment using the PEAS framework:

  • P — Performance Measure: The quantifiable metrics of success and penalties.
  • E — Environment: The physical or virtual external domain within which the agent operates.
  • A — Actuators: The control mechanisms through which the agent performs actions.
  • S — Sensors: The sensory instruments through which the agent collects data.

1.7 The Six Properties of Task Environments

Task environments vary dramatically. How we design an agent program depends directly on where the environment falls across these six dimensions:

1. Fully Observable vs. Partially Observable

If an agent's sensors give it access to the complete state of the environment at each point in time, it is fully observable. In such environments, the agent does not need to maintain internal memory to track what is happening elsewhere (e.g., Chess). If sensors are noisy, inaccurate, or parts of the world are occluded, it is partially observable (e.g., automated taxi driving; poker).

2. Deterministic vs. Stochastic

If the next state of the environment is completely determined by the current state and the action executed by the agent, the environment is deterministic (e.g., 8-puzzle). If uncertainty, random chance, or unpredictable factors play a role, it is stochastic (e.g., driving in traffic where tires may blow or pedestrians jump into the road). If the environment is deterministic except for the actions of other agents, it is called strategic.

3. Episodic vs. Sequential

In an episodic environment, the agent's experience is divided into atomic, independent episodes. Each episode consists of the agent perceiving and executing a single action; crucially, current actions do not affect future episodes (e.g., defect classification on an assembly line). In sequential environments, the current decision affects all future possibilities (e.g., Chess, driving).

4. Static vs. Dynamic

If the environment can change while the agent is deliberating, it is dynamic (e.g., driving a taxi—the world moves while you think!). If the environment remains frozen until the agent issues an action, it is static (e.g., crossword puzzles). If the environment itself doesn't change with time, but the agent's performance score does, it is called semidynamic (e.g., timed chess).

5. Discrete vs. Continuous

A discrete environment has a finite, countable number of distinct states, distinct percepts, and distinct actions (e.g., Chess board coordinates and turns). A continuous environment involves continuous sweeps of values over time, such as vehicle velocity, angles, and real coordinates (e.g., automated taxi).

6. Single Agent vs. Multi-Agent

An agent operating alone in an environment is in a single-agent setup (e.g., crossword puzzle, solitaire). When other entities act as agents (either competitive like chess or cooperative like multi-robot soccer), it is a multi-agent environment.

1.8 The Five Fundamental Agent Architectures

AI organizes agent programs into a progressive hierarchy based on increasing intelligence, memory, and deliberative capacity:

1. Simple Reflex Agents

Select actions based only on the current percept, ignoring the rest of the percept history. Built using condition-action rules: IF condition THEN action.

Critical Limitation: Only works if the environment is fully observable. In partially observable environments, simple reflex agents inevitably enter infinite loops.

2. Model-Based Reflex Agents

To overcome partial observability, this agent maintains an internal state that tracks aspects of the world it cannot currently see. It requires two forms of background knowledge:

  • Transition Model ("How the world evolves"): How the environment changes independently of the agent.
  • Sensor Model ("What my actions do"): How the agent's own actions impact the state.

3. Goal-Based Agents

Knowing the current state is not always sufficient to decide what to do. The agent needs goal information describing situations that are desirable (e.g., reaching destination B). Combines search and planning techniques (as we will study in Units 2 and 4).

4. Utility-Based Agents

Goals provide only a crude binary distinction between 'success' and 'failure'. A utility function maps a state onto a real number representing the degree of happiness or desirability. This allows the agent to make rational trade-offs between conflicting goals (e.g., speed vs. fuel efficiency vs. passenger safety).

5. Learning Agents

All previous architectures can be transformed into a learning agent. It is divided into four conceptual components:

  1. Learning Element: Responsible for making improvements based on feedback from the critic.
  2. Performance Element: The operational agent that selects external actions based on percepts.
  3. Critic: Evaluates how well the agent is doing with respect to an external performance standard.
  4. Problem Generator: Suggests exploratory actions that lead to new experiences, preventing the agent from being trapped in suboptimal habits.

SIMPLE Expected Score for a Vacuum-Cleaner Agent Action

GIVEN: A rational vacuum-cleaner agent in square A is deciding whether to execute action Suck.
  • Probability of dirt being successfully cleaned: \(p_1 = 0.85\); Reward = \(+100\).
  • Probability of mechanical sensor/motor failure (dirt remains): \(p_2 = 0.15\); Score = \(-10\) (energy spent without result).
FORMULA / PRINCIPLE OF MAXIMUM EXPECTED UTILITY:
\(E[U(Action)] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(Outcome_i \mid Action) \times U(Outcome_i)\)
STEPWISE WORKING:
  1. Identify probability and utility for outcome 1 (Success):
    \(P(Success) \times U(Success) = 0.85 \times 100 = 85.0\)
  2. Identify probability and utility for outcome 2 (Failure):
    \(P(Failure) \times U(Failure) = 0.15 \times (-10) = -1.5\)
  3. Sum the expected utilities:
    \(E[U(Suck)] = 85.0 + (-1.5) = 83.5\)
FINAL ANSWER: The expected utility of executing Suck is +83.5. Because 83.5 > 0, executing the action is strictly rational.

MEDIUM Autonomous Delivery Route Decision Under Traffic Uncertainty

GIVEN: An autonomous delivery robot must deliver medication between Hospital H and Clinic C. It has two route choices:
  • Route 1 (Highway): Fast but prone to traffic congestion.
    • Congestion occurs with probability \(0.30\) → Travel time = 50 mins; Cost = 50. Utility: \(U = -50\).
    • Clear traffic with probability \(0.70\) → Travel time = 15 mins; Cost = 15. Utility: \(U = -15\).
  • Route 2 (Inner City Lane): Reliable but consistently moderate speed.
    • Delays occur with probability \(0.10\) → Travel time = 35 mins; Utility: \(U = -35\).
    • Normal transit with probability \(0.90\) → Travel time = 24 mins; Utility: \(U = -24\).
FORMULA: \(E[U(Route)] = \sum P(Condition) \times U(TransitTime)\)
The rational agent selects: \(Route^* = \arg\max_{R} E[U(R)]\) (least negative cost).
STEPWISE WORKING:
  1. Calculate Expected Utility for Route 1 (Highway):
    \(E[U(Route_1)] = (0.70 \times -15) + (0.30 \times -50)\)
    \(E[U(Route_1)] = -10.5 - 15.0 = \mathbf{-25.5}\)
  2. Calculate Expected Utility for Route 2 (Inner City Lane):
    \(E[U(Route_2)] = (0.90 \times -24) + (0.10 \times -35)\)
    \(E[U(Route_2)] = -21.6 - 3.5 = \mathbf{-25.1}\)
  3. Comparison:
    \(-25.1 > -25.5\) (Route 2 incurs fewer average lost minutes).
FINAL ANSWER: The rational agent must select Route 2. Even though Route 1 has a faster best-case scenario (15 mins), its expected travel penalty (-25.5) is worse than Route 2 (-25.1).

COMPLEX Multi-Action Decision Analysis: Model-Based vs. Reflex Agent Under Sensor Noise

GIVEN: An inspection robot operates on a pipeline where a critical defect occurs with prior probability \(P(D) = 0.05\) (and normal state \(P(\neg D) = 0.95\)).
Sensors are noisy:
  • Sensor reports "Defect Detected" when a defect exists: \(P(S=1 \mid D) = 0.90\) (True Positive).
  • Sensor false alarm rate: \(P(S=1 \mid \neg D) = 0.08\) (False Positive).
Utility matrix of actions:
  • Action Shutdown Pipeline: Cost = -100 if Defect exists (prevented disaster); Cost = -500 if False Alarm (wasted production downtime).
  • Action Continue Operation: Utility = +50 if Normal; Catastrophic penalty = -5000 if Defect actually exists.
A Simple Reflex Agent triggers Shutdown immediately whenever \(S=1\). A Model-Based Rational Agent calculates the posterior probability \(P(D \mid S=1)\) via Bayes' Theorem before choosing the action.
FORMULAE:
  1. Bayes' Rule: \(P(D \mid S=1) = \frac{P(S=1 \mid D)P(D)}{P(S=1)}\)
  2. Total Probability: \(P(S=1) = P(S=1 \mid D)P(D) + P(S=1 \mid \neg D)P(\neg D)\)
  3. Expected Utility: \(E[U(a)] = P(D \mid S=1)U(a, D) + P(\neg D \mid S=1)U(a, \neg D)\)
STEPWISE WORKING:
  1. Calculate Total Probability of Sensor Alarm \(P(S=1)\):
    \(P(S=1) = (0.90 \times 0.05) + (0.08 \times 0.95) = 0.045 + 0.076 = 0.121\)
  2. Compute Posterior Probability of Actual Defect Given Alarm:
    \(P(D \mid S=1) = \frac{0.045}{0.121} \approx 0.3719\ (37.19\%)\)
    Consequently: \(P(\neg D \mid S=1) = 1 - 0.3719 = 0.6281\ (62.81\%)\)
  3. Compute Expected Utility for Action Shutdown:
    \(E[U(\text{Shutdown})] = (0.3719 \times -100) + (0.6281 \times -500)\)
    \(E[U(\text{Shutdown})] = -37.19 - 314.05 = \mathbf{-351.24}\)
  4. Compute Expected Utility for Action Continue:
    \(E[U(\text{Continue})] = (0.3719 \times -5000) + (0.6281 \times +50)\)
    \(E[U(\text{Continue})] = -1859.50 + 31.41 = \mathbf{-1828.09}\)
  5. Evaluation:
    Comparing \(E[U(\text{Shutdown})] = -351.24\) versus \(E[U(\text{Continue})] = -1828.09\).
    Even though there is only a 37.19% chance of an actual defect, the catastrophic penalty of continuing (-5000) makes Shutdown the mathematically rational choice.
FINAL ANSWER: The rational agent chooses Shutdown (\(E[U] = -351.24 > -1828.09\)). This demonstrates how a model-based agent balances probability against asymmetric utility consequences, preventing catastrophic failures.

4. Real-World Case Studies & Concrete Examples

Case 1: Autonomous Highway Driving System (Waymo / Tesla Autopilot)

  • Task Environment Analysis: Partially observable (blind spots, occlusion behind trucks), stochastic (pedestrians, mechanical tire failure), sequential (lane changes commit the vehicle to new trajectories), dynamic (vehicles move at 100 km/h during decision intervals), continuous (steering angle, velocity), multi-agent (competing and collaborating drivers).
  • Why Reflex Fails: A reflex rule "IF distance < 10m THEN Brake" will slam brakes on a highway when a vehicle briefly merges ahead, causing rear-end pileups.
  • Required Architecture: Model-based utility agent with learning. Must track other vehicles' velocity vectors in an internal occupancy grid, project future states, and optimize utility balancing safety, speed, and passenger comfort.

Case 2: Industrial Defect Inspection on Assembly Lines

  • Task Environment Analysis: Fully observable (high-speed strobe lighting over fixed conveyor belt), deterministic (parts move on fixed mechanical tracks), episodic (inspection of part 101 has zero effect on part 102), static (image captured and frozen for analysis), discrete class output (pass/reject), single agent.
  • Design Recommendation: A simple reflex or feedforward convolutional neural network is ideal. Because it is episodic and fully observable, maintaining an internal history between parts is completely unnecessary.
