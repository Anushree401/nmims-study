1.1 What is Artificial Intelligence? The Four Historical Approaches

Historically, researchers have organized definitions of Artificial Intelligence along two independent axes: Thought processes / Reasoning versus Behavior / Action, measured against either Human Performance (fidelity to human capability) or Ideal Rationality (adherence to an optimal normative standard called rationality).

Russell & Norvig's 2×2 Matrix of AI Definitions

Dimension Human-Centered Standard Ideal Rational Standard (Normative)
Thinking / Reasoning Thinking Humanly: Cognitive Science approach. Validating computational models via neuroimaging and psychological reaction-time experiments. Thinking Rationally: Laws of Thought approach. Aristotle's syllogistic logic: codifying correct, irrefutable reasoning patterns.
Acting / Behavior Acting Humanly: Turing Test approach (Alan Turing, 1950). Acting so convincingly like a human that an interrogator cannot tell the difference. Acting Rationally: Rational Agent approach (Modern AI standard). Operating autonomously to maximize expected utility given available percepts.

Detailed Examination of the 4 Approaches:

  1. Acting Humanly (The Turing Test Approach): Proposed by Alan Turing in 1950 in his seminal paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence. Turing replaced the philosophical question "Can machines think?" with an empirical behavioral game: the Imitation Game. A computer passes if a human interrogator asking written questions cannot distinguish whether responses come from a human or a machine. To pass the complete Turing Test, an AI must master six core disciplines:
    • Natural Language Processing (NLP): To communicate successfully in conversational English.
    • Knowledge Representation: To store what it knows and hears.
    • Automated Reasoning: To answer queries and draw sound conclusions using stored knowledge.
    • Machine Learning: To adapt to new circumstances and detect patterns.
    • Computer Vision (Total Turing Test): To perceive visual objects.
    • Robotics (Total Turing Test): To manipulate physical objects and navigate physical environments.
  2. Thinking Humanly (The Cognitive Modeling Approach): Seeks to understand how human minds physically operate. Approaches include: (a) introspection (catching our own thoughts as they occur), (b) psychological experiments on human subjects, and (c) brain imaging (fMRI/EEG). Modern Cognitive Science combines AI computing algorithms with experimental psychology techniques to construct testable theories of the human mind.
  3. Thinking Rationally (The Laws of Thought Approach): Founded upon Aristotle's classic syllogisms (e.g., "Socrates is a man; all men are mortal; therefore, Socrates is mortal"). Initiated the logicist tradition in AI. However, this approach faces two major obstacles:
    • It is exceedingly difficult to formalize informal, ambiguous common-sense knowledge into crisp first-order logic sentences.
    • There is a vast difference between solving a problem in principle and solving it in practice—computational intractability arises quickly as state space grows.
  4. Acting Rationally (The Rational Agent Approach - The Modern AI Standard): A rational agent is one that acts so as to achieve the best outcome or, when there is uncertainty, the best expected outcome. Doing what is right given what the agent knows. Acting rationally subsumes logical reasoning (an agent must often reason logically to achieve its goals), but also covers situations where logic is insufficient (e.g., reflex reactions where deliberative deduction takes too long, or acting under severe probability uncertainty).

1.2 The Relationship: AI vs. Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

A common conceptual confusion is conflating Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning. They are hierarchically nested subsets:

Figure 1.1: Hierarchical nesting of Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning.

  • Artificial Intelligence: The overarching discipline born at Dartmouth in 1956. Encompasses symbolic reasoning, expert systems, search heuristics, constraint satisfaction, knowledge graphs, and learning systems.
  • Machine Learning: A subfield of AI focused on algorithms that learn predictive mapping functions \(h(x) \approx y\) directly from empirical training datasets without explicit rule programming.
  • Deep Learning: A specialized subfield of Machine Learning utilizing deep artificial neural network architectures (hundreds of stacked layers) capable of automatic end-to-end representation learning from unstructured data (images, raw audio, text).

1.3 What is an Agent? Rationality vs. Omniscience

An agent is anything that can be viewed as perceiving its environment through sensors and acting upon that environment through actuators.

Mathematical Agent Function vs. Agent Program

An agent's behavior is mathematically described by the Agent Function \(f\), which maps any given percept sequence to an action:

$$f: \mathcal{P}^* \to \mathcal{A}$$

where \(\mathcal{P}^*\) is the set of all possible historical percept sequences experienced up to current time \(t\), and \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of candidate actions.

The Agent Program is the concrete software/hardware implementation of function \(f\) executing on an underlying physical architecture: \(\mathbf{\text{Agent} = \text{Architecture} + \text{Program}}\).

Rationality is NOT Omniscience:

  • Omniscience: An omniscient agent knows the actual outcome of its actions and can act accordingly; omniscience is impossible in reality.
  • Rationality: A rational agent maximizes expected performance given its historical percept sequence and built-in knowledge. For example, if an autonomous vehicle looks both ways, sees a clear street, moves forward, and is hit by an airplane falling from the sky, the agent was rational (not negligent), even though the outcome was catastrophic.
  • Exploration & Learning: A rational agent must not merely react; it must engage in information gathering (exploration) and update its internal world model from experience (learning) to achieve true autonomy.

1.4 Task Environments & PEAS Formulation

In designing any rational agent, the very first engineering step is to formally specify its Task Environment using the PEAS framework:

  • P – Performance Measure: The objective numerical or qualitative criterion used to judge how successful an agent is. (Must be designed carefully based on what is actually desired in the environment, rather than how the agent behaves).
  • E – Environment: The external world or context with which the agent interacts.
  • A – Actuators: The mechanisms, motors, displays, or interfaces through which the agent executes actions upon the environment.
  • S – Sensors: The sensory devices, cameras, microphones, or network buffers through which the agent receives percepts.

Canonical PEAS Examples (Exam-Ready Matrix):

Agent Type Performance Measure (P) Environment (E) Actuators (A) Sensors (S)
Automated Taxi Driver Safe, fast, legal trip, maximum profit, passenger comfort Roads, traffic, pedestrians, weather, customers Steering wheel, accelerator, brakes, horn, display Cameras, LiDAR, radar, GPS, speedometer, odometer
Medical Diagnostic System Healthy patient, minimized healthcare costs, zero misdiagnoses Patient, hospital staff, laboratory facilities Screen display, prescription printer, referral alerts Keyboard (symptoms, patient history, lab readings)
Automated Vacuum Cleaner Cleanliness, time efficiency, battery preservation, low noise Room floors, carpet, furniture, obstacles, dirt Wheels, suction motor, brushing arms Bump sensors, infrared cliff detectors, dirt optical sensor
ATM Machine Agent Zero cash errors, transaction speed, fraud prevention, uptime Bank customer, banking network, cash vault Cash dispenser, receipt printer, card ejector, screen Card reader, PIN keypad, touchscreen, optical vault sensors

1.5 Properties / Dimensions of Task Environments

Task environments vary dramatically across six fundamental computational dimensions, directly dictating agent design complexity:

  1. Fully Observable vs. Partially Observable:
    • Fully Observable: The agent's sensors give complete access to the full state of the environment at each point in time (e.g., Chess, Crossword puzzles). No internal memory of past states is needed to determine the true current state.
    • Partially Observable: Sensors are noisy, incomplete, or obstructed (e.g., Automated driving, Poker where opponents' cards are hidden). The agent must maintain an internal world model.
  2. Deterministic vs. Stochastic:
    • Deterministic: The next state of the environment is completely determined by the current state and the action executed by the agent (e.g., Chess, Tic-Tac-Toe).
    • Stochastic: Randomness, physical slip, or unpredictable external events introduce probability distributions over outcomes (e.g., Backgammon with dice rolls, Taxi driving).
  3. Episodic vs. Sequential:
    • Episodic: The agent's experience is divided into independent atomic episodes. Current action choice does not affect whether future episodes are easy or difficult (e.g., Defect classification on a factory conveyor belt).
    • Sequential: Current decisions affect all future decisions and states (e.g., Chess, Maze navigation). The agent must engage in long-range planning.
  4. Static vs. Dynamic:
    • Static: The environment does not change while the agent is deliberating and computing its next action (e.g., Crossword puzzle).
    • Dynamic: The environment continuously changes while the agent decides what to do (e.g., Taxi driving, algorithmic trading).
    • Semidynamic: The environment itself does not change with time, but the agent's performance score does (e.g., Chess played with a timed clock).
  5. Discrete vs. Continuous:
    • Discrete: A finite, distinct number of states, percepts, and actions (e.g., Chess board coordinates, Tic-Tac-Toe).
    • Continuous: Real-valued continuous variables (e.g., Speed, steering angles, continuous physical coordinates in robotics).
  6. Single Agent vs. Multi-Agent:
    • Single Agent: An agent operating alone in an environment (e.g., Crossword puzzle, Solitaire).
    • Multi-Agent: Multiple entities interacting:
      • Competitive Multi-Agent: Opponents trying to maximize opposite utility functions (e.g., Chess - zero sum).
      • Cooperative Multi-Agent: Teammates coordinating to achieve a shared goal (e.g., Multi-robot search and rescue).

1.6 The Five Intelligent Agent Architecture Archetypes

Russell & Norvig classify intelligent agent programs into five distinct architectural patterns of increasing sophistication:

Figure 1.2: Comparative architectures of Simple Reflex, Model-Based, and Utility-Based Agents.

Detailed Architectures:

  1. Simple Reflex Agent: Selects actions based only on the current percept, completely ignoring percept history. Operates via Condition-Action rules (e.g., IF car-ahead-brakes THEN apply-brakes).
    • Limitation: Succeeds only if the environment is fully observable. If partially observable, simple reflex agents inevitably enter unrecoverable infinite loops.
  2. Model-Based Reflex Agent: Maintains an internal state to track unobserved aspects of the current world. It relies on two models:
    • Transition Model: Knowledge of how the world evolves independently of the agent over time.
    • Sensor Model: Knowledge of how the state of the world reflects into the agent's percepts.
  3. Goal-Based Agent: Combines internal state information with a formal Goal description (a desirable destination state). Uses search and planning (studied in Unit 2) to compute action sequences that achieve the goal. Far more flexible than reflex rules (if goals change, simply alter the goal specification rather than rewriting hundreds of condition-action rules).
  4. Utility-Based Agent: Goals provide only a binary distinction between success and failure. A Utility Function \(U(s)\) maps a state to a real number indicating the agent's degree of happiness or performance. Allows rational decisions in three crucial situations:
    • When goals conflict (e.g., speed vs safety).
    • When multiple goals exist and none can be achieved with certainty (weighing likelihood of success vs importance).
    • When tradeoffs between cost and benefit must be made.
  5. Learning Agent: Divides the agent into four distinct conceptual components:
    • Learning Element: Responsible for making improvements and learning from experience.
    • Performance Element: Responsible for selecting external actions (takes percepts and decides actions).
    • Critic: Evaluates the agent's behavior against an external performance standard and provides feedback.
    • Problem Generator: Suggests exploratory actions that lead to new, informative experiences (sub-optimal in short term, but necessary for long-term optimization).

Simple Example

Problem 1.1: Expected Utility Calculation for Automated Cleaning Agent

Problem Statement: An autonomous vacuum cleaner agent has dirty floor in square A. If it chooses action Suck, the actuator succeeds with probability \(0.85\) (yielding a performance utility of \(+100\)), but fails with probability \(0.15\) (clogging the motor, yielding a penalty utility of \(-10\)). Calculate the Expected Utility \(\mathbb{E}[U(\text{Suck})]\) of executing this action.

GIVEN

Outcome 1 (Success): \(p_1 = 0.85\), \(U(O_1) = +100\).

Outcome 2 (Failure): \(p_2 = 0.15\), \(U(O_2) = -10\).

FORMULA
$$\mathbb{E}[U(a)] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(O_i \mid a) \cdot U(O_i)$$
STEPS

Step 1: Compute weighted utility of each possible outcome:

$$\text{Term}_1 = 0.85 \times 100 = 85.0$$ $$\text{Term}_2 = 0.15 \times (-10) = -1.5$$

Step 2: Sum the expectations:

$$\mathbb{E}[U(\text{Suck})] = 85.0 + (-1.5) = 83.5$$
FINAL ANSWER

\(\mathbf{\mathbb{E}[U(\text{Suck})] = +83.5 \text{ utility units}}\)

Decision Interpretation: Since \(\mathbb{E}[U(\text{Suck})] = 83.5 > 0\), a rational utility agent will proceed with the action.

Medium Example

Problem 1.2: Rational Route Selection Under Probability Uncertainty

Problem Statement: An autonomous delivery robot must choose between two routes, Highway (\(R_H\)) and Local Roads (\(R_L\)), to deliver medical supplies:

  • Highway (\(R_H\)): Subject to unpredictable traffic jams. Probability of heavy traffic is \(0.30\) (utility \(= -50\)); probability of clear highway is \(0.70\) (utility \(= -15\)).
  • Local Roads (\(R_L\)): Reliable but slower. Probability of construction delay is \(0.10\) (utility \(= -35\)); probability of normal flow is \(0.90\) (utility \(= -22\)).

Determine which route a Rational Utility-Maximizing Agent will choose, and calculate the expected utility margin.

GIVEN

Route Highway (\(R_H\)): \(P(\text{Jam}) = 0.30, U = -50\); \(P(\text{Clear}) = 0.70, U = -15\).

Route Local (\(R_L\)): \(P(\text{Delay}) = 0.10, U = -35\); \(P(\text{Normal}) = 0.90, U = -22\).

FORMULA
$$\text{Optimal Action } a^* = \arg\max_{a} \mathbb{E}[U(a)]$$
STEPS

Step 1: Compute Expected Utility of Highway Route:

$$\mathbb{E}[U(R_H)] = (0.30 \times -50) + (0.70 \times -15) = -15.0 - 10.5 = -25.5$$

Step 2: Compute Expected Utility of Local Roads Route:

$$\mathbb{E}[U(R_L)] = (0.10 \times -35) + (0.90 \times -22) = -3.5 - 19.8 = -23.3$$

Step 3: Compare expectations:

$$-23.3 > -25.5 \implies \mathbb{E}[U(R_L)] > \mathbb{E}[U(R_H)]$$
FINAL ANSWER

\(\mathbf{\text{Rational Choice: Local Roads } (R_L) \text{ with } \mathbb{E}[U] = -23.3}\)

The local road route is superior because its higher reliability yields a lower expected cost penalty (difference of \(2.2\) utility units).

Complex Example

Problem 1.3: Model-Based Decision Under Bayesian Sensor Noise

Problem Statement: A pipeline monitoring robot inspects welds. A structural defect exists with prior probability \(P(D) = 0.05\) (normal state \(P(\neg D) = 0.95\)). Its ultrasonic sensor is noisy:

  • True Positive Rate: \(P(S=1 \mid D) = 0.90\)
  • False Positive Rate: \(P(S=1 \mid \neg D) = 0.08\)

The robot can choose between two actions:

  • Emergency Shutdown (\(a_1\)): Utility is \(-100\) if defect exists (averted disaster); Utility is \(-500\) if false alarm (needless factory stoppage).
  • Continue Operation (\(a_2\)): Utility is \(-5000\) if defect exists (catastrophic pipeline rupture); Utility is \(0\) if no defect.

If the sensor triggers an alarm (\(S=1\)), determine the rational action by computing the posterior defect probability \(P(D \mid S=1)\) and comparing expected utilities.

GIVEN

Priors: \(P(D) = 0.05\), \(P(\neg D) = 0.95\).

Sensor Probabilities: \(P(S=1 \mid D) = 0.90\), \(P(S=1 \mid \neg D) = 0.08\).

FORMULAE
$$P(S=1) = P(S=1 \mid D)P(D) + P(S=1 \mid \neg D)P(\neg D)$$ $$P(D \mid S=1) = \frac{P(S=1 \mid D)P(D)}{P(S=1)}$$ $$\mathbb{E}[U(a)] = P(D \mid S=1)U(a, D) + P(\neg D \mid S=1)U(a, \neg D)$$
STEPS

Step 1: Compute Total Probability of Sensor Alarm \(P(S=1)\):

$$P(S=1) = (0.90 \times 0.05) + (0.08 \times 0.95) = 0.045 + 0.076 = 0.121$$

Step 2: Compute Posterior Probability of Real Defect \(P(D \mid S=1)\):

$$P(D \mid S=1) = \frac{0.045}{0.121} \approx 0.3719 \quad (37.19\%)$$ $$P(\neg D \mid S=1) = 1 - 0.3719 = 0.6281 \quad (62.81\%)$$

Step 3: Compute Expected Utility of Action \(a_1\) (Emergency Shutdown):

$$\mathbb{E}[U(a_1)] = (0.3719 \times -100) + (0.6281 \times -500) = -37.19 - 314.05 = -351.24$$

Step 4: Compute Expected Utility of Action \(a_2\) (Continue Operation):

$$\mathbb{E}[U(a_2)] = (0.3719 \times -5000) + (0.6281 \times 0) = -1859.50$$
FINAL ANSWER

Posterior Defect Probability: \(P(D \mid S=1) = 37.19\%\)

Expected Utilities: \(\mathbb{E}[U(a_1)] = -351.24\) vs \(\mathbb{E}[U(a_2)] = -1859.50\)

Rational Decision: \(\mathbf{\text{Execute Emergency Shutdown } (a_1)}\)

Crucial Exam Insight: Even though the probability of a false alarm is higher than an actual defect (62.8% vs 37.2%), the catastrophic cost of a rupture makes shutdown the rational decision.

4. Real-World Case Studies & Practical Systems

Autonomous Real-World Implementations
Autonomous Robotics Case Study

Waymo Autonomous Driver (Alphabet / Google)

System Profile: Waymo operates commercial fully driverless robotaxis across major US metropolitan areas without a human safety driver.

  • PEAS Formalization:
    • P (Performance): Zero collisions, passenger ETA, ride smoothness, strict traffic law compliance.
    • E (Environment): High-density urban streets, aggressive human drivers, jaywalking pedestrians, heavy rain.
    • A (Actuators): Steer-by-wire actuators, hydraulic brake pressure, electric motor acceleration.
    • S (Sensors): 360-degree LiDAR pulses, long-range radar arrays, high-resolution cameras, audio sirens.
  • Agent Architecture: Implements a Utility-Based Learning Agent architecture. It maintains an internal 3D dynamic world model (predicting pedestrian trajectory vectors 5 seconds into the future) and computes trajectories that maximize safety and passenger comfort.
Game AI Case Study

DeepMind AlphaGo / AlphaZero

System Profile: In 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated 18-time world champion Lee Sedol in Go (\(19 \times 19\) board with state space \(10^{170}\)).

  • Environment Profile: Fully Observable, Deterministic, Sequential, Static, Discrete, Multi-Agent (Competitive Zero-Sum).
  • Rational Action Strategy: Combined Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with deep policy and value networks, moving beyond human heuristic rules to discover novel strategies that altered centuries of traditional Go theory.

5. Possible University Exam Questions & Marking Guide

NMIMS Pattern: 4, 6, 8, 10 Marks
4 Marks

Q1. Define a Rational Agent. How does Rationality differ from Omniscience?

Marking Scheme: 2 marks for formal definition of rational agent; 2 marks for clear distinction between rationality and omniscience.

Model Answer Key: A rational agent is one that acts so as to achieve the best expected outcome based on its historical percept sequence and built-in knowledge: \(f: \mathcal{P}^* \to \mathcal{A}\). Omniscience means knowing the actual outcome in advance, which is impossible in uncertain real-world environments. Rationality is about making the best decision given available information, not achieving perfection.

6 Marks

Q2. State the PEAS description for (a) An Automated Taxi Driver and (b) An Automated Vacuum Cleaner.

Marking Scheme: 3 marks for Taxi Driver (P, E, A, S); 3 marks for Vacuum Cleaner (P, E, A, S).

Model Answer Key: Provide the 4-tuple components as structured in Table 1.1: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, and Sensors for each system.

8 Marks

Q3. With the aid of neat block diagrams, explain the architecture of (a) Simple Reflex Agent and (b) Model-Based Reflex Agent. Why do simple reflex agents fail in partially observable environments?

Marking Scheme: 3 marks for architecture diagrams; 3 marks for component explanations; 2 marks for explaining failure in partially observable environments.

Model Answer Key: Draw Figure 1.2. Explain that simple reflex agents only check current percepts against condition-action rules. In partially observable environments, two identical percepts may require different actions depending on history, causing the simple reflex agent to enter infinite cycles.

10 Marks

Q4. Classify task environments along six distinct dimensions with examples. Explain the structure and working of a Learning Agent.

Marking Scheme: 5 marks for 6 environment dimensions with examples; 5 marks for 4 components of Learning Agent (Critic, Learning Element, Performance Element, Problem Generator).

Model Answer Key: Detail Observable, Deterministic, Episodic, Static, Discrete, and Single vs Multi-agent. Then detail the four learning agent components and how the problem generator creates exploratory actions.
