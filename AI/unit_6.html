1. In-depth theory explanation

Related: worked examples · comparative studies · case studies

1. Definition and model

  • An expert system (ES) is a program that uses explicit, domain-specific knowledge and inference procedures to solve a bounded class of problems.
  • A useful model is Expert system = Knowledge base + inference/problem-solving methods.
  • It aims to reproduce selected expert reasoning, not general human intelligence.

2. Architecture and components

  • Knowledge base: factual knowledge accepted in domain and heuristic know-how/judgment.
  • Fact database / working memory: case-specific facts for the current consultation.
  • Inference engine: matches rules to facts, derives conclusions, handles rule conflicts; may use forward or backward chaining.
  • User interface: collects facts and presents recommendations.
  • Explanation facility: explains which rules/facts support a conclusion or why it asks a question.
  • Knowledge-base editor/acquisition tools: let knowledge engineers capture, update and test knowledge.
  • People: domain expert supplies problem expertise; knowledge engineer represents and implements it; end user’s workflow and explanation needs shape design.

3. Characteristics, strengths and limits

  • ES separates knowledge from control program, uses symbolic representations, can explain its reasoning, and focuses on a narrow domain.
  • It can advise, diagnose, interpret, predict, justify conclusions and suggest alternatives.
  • Benefits include consistent application of rules, speed, availability and preservation of scarce expertise.
  • Limits: it cannot replace human judgment in all situations; performance is bounded by knowledge quality and coverage; ordinary rule systems do not automatically learn/refine their knowledge; expert knowledge is difficult and costly to elicit; maintenance is essential; common sense and novel situations are weak points.
  • A confident explanation does not guarantee a correct conclusion.

4. Rule reasoning and certainty factors

  • Rule-based ES encode knowledge as IF conditions THEN conclusion.
  • Forward chaining starts from facts and fires matching rules; backward chaining starts from a hypothesis and seeks supporting evidence.
  • If multiple rules fire, conflict resolution can use priority, specificity or recency.
  • MYCIN certainty factors (CF) quantify degree of support/confirmation, not Bayesian probability.
  • A simplified classroom rule may attach CF=0.8 to “evidence supports hypothesis.” Combine independent positive CFs (both positive) using CF₁₂=CF₁+CF₂(1−CF₁).

Simple CF calculation (illustrative)

  • One rule supports H with CF=0.70 and premise certainty 0.80.
  • Rule contribution is 0.70×0.80=0.56.

Medium: combine two positive supports

  • Rule A supports H with CF₁=0.60; independent rule B with CF₂=0.50.
  1. Remaining uncertainty after first support = 1−0.60=0.40.
  2. Additional support contributes 0.50×0.40=0.20.
  3. Combined CF=0.60+0.20=0.80.

Complex: chain and combine (teaching example)

  • Rule 1: evidence E supports intermediate I with CF 0.8; observed E has certainty 0.9.
  • Rule 2: I supports H with CF 0.7; independent direct evidence supports H at CF 0.4.
  • Use the simplified positive CF combination convention.
  1. Rule 1 contribution to I = 0.8×0.9=0.72.
  2. Propagate through Rule 2 to H = 0.72×0.7=0.504.
  3. Combine with direct CF 0.4: 0.504+0.4(1−0.504)=0.504+0.1984=0.7024.
  4. Interpret only as CF support under the stated simplified rule; CF is not Bayesian probability. Real MYCIN used more detailed combination rules for positive/negative evidence.

6. Development process

  1. Choose a bounded, suitable problem domain; confirm expert knowledge exists and value justifies cost.
  2. Identify domain expert, knowledge engineer, users and success criteria.
  3. Select representation, inference strategy, tools/shell and integration requirements.
  4. Acquire knowledge; represent facts/rules/heuristics in the KB.
  5. Build a prototype and explanation mechanism.
  6. Test with known cases, edge cases and end users; identify missing/contradictory rules.
  7. Refine iteratively, validate performance, document and deploy.
  8. Train users and maintain/update the KB as domain knowledge and interfaces change.
  • A shell supplies reusable inference, UI and explanation facilities without the domain-specific knowledge base; developers add the domain rules.

Lesson A · Why an expert system is more than a set of IF statements

  • A normal program can contain decision logic, but an expert system’s defining design is that domain knowledge is represented explicitly and separated from the general reasoning mechanism.
  • This separation lets a knowledge engineer update a rule without rewriting the entire inference engine, and lets the system explain which evidence and rules led to its recommendation.
  • Scope is deliberately narrow.
  • A system for troubleshooting one class of equipment may encode symptoms, failure modes and diagnostic tests.
  • It is not expected to understand unrelated domains or common-sense context.
  • Expert-system quality depends on whether the encoded knowledge is accurate, complete enough, current and applied to facts that truly describe the case.

Lesson B · Follow a consultation through the architecture

  1. The user supplies case facts through the interface; uncertain or missing facts may trigger questions.
  2. Facts enter working memory, separate from general domain rules.
  3. The inference engine finds rules whose conditions match those facts.
  4. It fires a rule, adds a conclusion, and repeats until a recommendation or stopping condition is reached.
  5. The explanation facility presents the supporting chain: “I concluded X because facts A and B satisfied rule R.” It may also explain why it asked a question.
  6. A knowledge engineer updates rules through a knowledge-base editor; domain experts validate their correctness; end users assess whether the workflow is usable.
  • The knowledge base may contain factual knowledge (widely accepted statements) and heuristic knowledge (expert rules of thumb).
  • A heuristic can be useful without being universally true, so its scope and confidence should be explicit.

Lesson C · Rule inference and certainty factors

  • A rule has an IF condition (antecedent) and THEN conclusion (consequent).
  • Forward chaining starts from case facts and derives what follows.
  • Backward chaining starts with a proposed conclusion and asks what facts would support it.
  • If several rules apply, conflict resolution may prioritize a more specific rule, an explicit priority, or recently added evidence.
  • MYCIN used certainty factors to represent the strength of evidence for or against a hypothesis.
  • They are not ordinary probabilities.
  • For a simplified positive-positive combination, CF₁₂=CF₁+CF₂(1−CF₁).
  • For example, CF 0.6 plus CF 0.5 yields 0.6+0.5(0.4)=0.8.
  • This formula represents diminishing extra support as confidence grows.

Three levels of calculation

  • Simple: a rule has strength 0.7 and its premise is believed with certainty 0.8.
  • Rule contribution = 0.7×0.8=0.56.
  • Medium: two independent positive rules support H at 0.60 and 0.50.
  • Combined CF =0.60+0.50(1−0.60)=0.80.
  • Complex chained example: evidence E has certainty 0.90; Rule 1 supports intermediate I with strength 0.80; Rule 2 supports H from I with strength 0.70; direct evidence also supports H at 0.40.
  1. Contribution to I: 0.90×0.80=0.72.
  2. Propagate I through Rule 2: 0.72×0.70=0.504 support for H.
  3. Combine with direct support: 0.504+0.40(1−0.504)=0.504+0.1984=0.7024.
  4. State that this is a simplified positive-evidence CF calculation. MYCIN’s full calculus handles positive and negative evidence with additional rules; do not label 0.7024 as a Bayesian probability.

Lesson E · Building an expert system iteratively

  • Development begins by checking domain suitability: decisions recur, expertise can be articulated, the task is bounded, and the expected benefit justifies development and maintenance.
  • A domain expert knows the task; a knowledge engineer knows representations and tools; users define practical workflow requirements.
  1. Scope: define problem, users, inputs, outputs and success criteria.
  2. Acquire: interview experts, examine cases, manuals and exceptions; distinguish facts from heuristics.
  3. Represent: choose rules, frames, logic or another representation; specify uncertainty and conflict policy.
  4. Prototype: implement a small rule base and working consultation path.
  5. Test: known cases, boundary cases, contradictory facts, missing information and user trials.
  6. Refine: correct gaps, conflicts, bad questions and explanations; repeat.
  7. Integrate/deploy: verify interfaces and databases, document and train users.
  8. Maintain: review rules as domain knowledge and connected systems change.
  • An expert-system shell supplies reusable inference, interface and explanation capabilities without a domain knowledge base.
  • A development tool may include editors and debugging support.
  • The shell still needs carefully elicited and validated domain knowledge.

Certainty-factor (CF) calculations are not ordinary probability calculations. Write the CF rule used, show premise strength, and label the final result as a CF.

SIMPLE NUMERICAL Apply one rule with uncertain evidence

GIVEN
  • Rule support for H: CF(rule)=0.70.
  • Premise certainty: CF(premise)=0.80.
FORMULA / RULE
Rule contribution = CF(rule) × CF(premise).
STEPWISE WORKING
  1. Multiply rule strength by premise certainty: .70×.80.
  2. Compute .70×.80=.56.
FINAL ANSWER
Contribution to H=CF 0.56. See certainty factors.

MEDIUM NUMERICAL Combine two positive pieces of support

GIVEN
  • Rule 1 supports H with CF₁=.60.
  • Rule 2 supports H with CF₂=.50.
FORMULA / RULE
For two positive CFs: CF₁₂=CF₁+CF₂(1−CF₁).
STEPWISE WORKING
  1. Remaining uncertainty after the first support is 1−.60=.40.
  2. Second support contributes .50×.40=.20.
  3. Combined CF=.60+.20=.80.
FINAL ANSWER
Combined support is CF 0.80, not a claim of 80% probability. See expert systems vs conventional programs.

COMPLEX NUMERICAL Chain two rules, then combine direct evidence

GIVEN
  • Evidence E has certainty .90.
  • Rule 1: E supports intermediate I with strength .80.
  • Rule 2: I supports hypothesis H with strength .70.
  • Direct evidence separately supports H with CF .40.
FORMULA / RULE
Rule contribution = premise CF × rule CF. Combine positive CFs using CF₁₂=CF₁+CF₂(1−CF₁).
STEPWISE WORKING
  1. Evidence supports I: .90×.80=.72.
  2. Propagate I to H: .72×.70=.504.
  3. Combine with direct CF .40: .504+.40(1−.504).
  4. Compute remaining factor: 1−.504=.496.
  5. Direct contribution=.40×.496=.1984.
  6. Combined CF=.504+.1984=.7024.
FINAL ANSWER
Combined support for H=CF 0.7024. It is not a Bayesian probability. See MYCIN.

5. Case study: MYCIN

  • MYCIN was a Stanford rule-based medical expert system developed in the 1970s for diagnosing serious bacterial infections (including blood infections/meningitis) and advising antibiotic treatment.
  • It asked clinicians for case facts, matched them against rules, ranked candidate organisms and recommendations, and used certainty factors to represent uncertain support.
  • Its separation of domain rules from inference helped inspire expert-system shells such as EMYCIN.
  • What the case demonstrates: narrow domain + explicit rules + interactive evidence gathering + explanation can make a useful decision-support tool.
  • What it does not prove: autonomous replacement of clinicians.
  • Knowledge acquisition, data quality, clinical context, responsibility and deployment constraints remain central.
  • Cite MYCIN as a historical case rather than a currently deployed clinical system.

Lesson D · MYCIN as a case study

  • MYCIN was developed at Stanford in the 1970s to assist with diagnosis and treatment recommendations for serious bacterial infections.
  • It interactively collected case facts, applied a large rule base, ranked candidate organisms and suggested therapy.
  • Its use of certainty factors addressed the fact that medical clues are rarely absolutely conclusive.
  • The example demonstrates a knowledge base, inference engine, consultation interface and explanation mechanism working together.
  • Its historical importance is also in what it exposed: acquiring expertise from clinicians is difficult; medical knowledge changes; incomplete or incorrect facts affect output; and a plausible recommendation is not a substitute for clinical responsibility.
  • MYCIN was a research decision-support system, not evidence that software can autonomously replace physicians.
  • The supplied slides identify the case and CF approach; linked Stanford sources in the reference list document the historical example.

5. Possible exam questions

8. Possible questions

  1. Define expert system and explain its model and architecture with diagram.
  2. Explain knowledge base, working memory, inference engine, interface and explanation facility.
  3. Discuss characteristics, strengths and limitations.
  4. Compare forward and backward chaining in rule-based systems.
  5. Explain MYCIN and certainty factors; distinguish CF from probability.
  6. Describe the iterative development lifecycle and roles of domain expert/knowledge engineer/user.
  7. What is an expert-system shell? Explain its benefits.
  8. For 10 marks, assess MYCIN as a case study: architecture, reasoning, contribution and limits.

Lesson G · Exam answer structure

  • For architecture, draw the user → interface → inference engine ↔ knowledge base flow and add working memory plus explanation.
  • For development, narrate the iterative cycle from scope to knowledge acquisition, prototype, test, refinement and maintenance.
  • For MYCIN, connect historical task, rules, interaction, certainty factors, benefit and limitation.
  • In a CF numerical, show the rule contribution before combining it, write the formula and clarify why a CF is not automatically a probability.
