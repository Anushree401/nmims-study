1. In-Depth Theory & Lecture Explanation

Faithful to Colour Image Processing PDF & Syllabus

1.1 Color Models & Spaces

  • RGB Model (Additive Primaries): Used in active light-emitting displays (monitors, phone screens). Colors are formed by adding Red, Green, and Blue light. Represented as a 3D unit cube.
  • CMY / CMYK Model (Subtractive Primaries): Used in commercial printing devices. Cyan, Magenta, and Yellow pigments absorb (subtract) light. Black (\(K\)) is added for true deep dark tones and ink cost savings: $$\begin{bmatrix} C \\ M \\ Y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix}$$
  • HSI Model (Hue, Saturation, Intensity): Decouples color chromaticity from luminance. Directly reflects human perceptual color descriptions:
    • Hue (\(H\)): Represents the pure dominant spectral color (angle from 0° to 360°).
    • Saturation (\(S\)): Represents purity or dilution with white light (radius from 0 to 1).
    • Intensity (\(I\)): Represents perceived brightness/lightness (vertical axis from 0 to 1).
  • YUV / YCbCr Models: Used in analog and digital television broadcast (PAL/NTSC) and JPEG compression. Separates luminance (\(Y\)) from color chrominance (\(U, V\)), allowing chrominance subsampling (e.g., 4:2:0) to exploit human visual insensitivity to color details!

1.2 Mathematical Conversion: RGB to HSI

Given normalized RGB values in \([0, 1]\):

$$\mathbf{I = \frac{1}{3} (R + G + B)}$$ $$\mathbf{S = 1 - \frac{3}{(R + G + B)} [\min(R, G, B)]}$$ $$\mathbf{H = \begin{cases} \theta & \text{if } B \le G \\ 360^\circ - \theta & \text{if } B > G \end{cases} \quad \text{where } \theta = \cos^{-1}\left( \frac{\frac{1}{2}[(R-G) + (R-B)]}{\sqrt{(R-G)^2 + (R-B)(G-B)}} \right)}$$

Undefined hue case: When \(R=G=B\), the denominator in the hue formula is zero; a gray pixel has no hue. Use the course convention if a numeric hue must be stored.

1.3 Colour Slicing, Model Conversion, and Colour Histograms

Colour slicing highlights a selected range in a colour space. A simple RGB slice tests whether each channel lies within chosen bounds; perceptually meaningful selection is often easier in HSV/HSI, where hue and saturation can be thresholded separately. Pixels outside the selected range can be suppressed or shown in a neutral background.

Model conversion changes representation, not the underlying scene: transform each pixel using the source-to-target equations, respect channel ranges and conventions, and clamp/quantize only when converting to a bounded digital representation. RGB-to-HSI equations are given above; inverse conversion is piecewise by hue sector (0–120°, 120–240°, or 240–360°).

A colour histogram may be computed per channel or jointly across colour components. Per-channel histogram equalization is simple but can shift colours because each channel is remapped independently; luminance/intensity-only enhancement can better preserve chromatic relationships.

Final Revision Anchor

Core Exam Takeaways:

  • RGB vs CMY: Additive (light emission) vs Subtractive (ink printing). \(\text{CMY} = 1 - \text{RGB}\).
  • HSI Superiority: Decouples chromaticity (\(H, S\)) from intensity (\(I\)); ideal for computer vision color segmentation.
  • YUV Subsampling: Human eyes have high spatial rods (luminance \(Y\)) and low color cones (\(U, V\)). Subsampling saves 50% bandwidth without perceptual loss.
